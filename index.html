Far from home.

Four international students. Four ways to build a community without leaving other homes behind.

I listened to four international students describe their first impressions of Lubbock, the people they trust, and the places they return to. Their stories do not agree on what counts as home. Some value the university but miss family elsewhere; another finds home in relationships more than in the city itself. That distinction leads this investigation: Can someone feel at home with people without feeling at home in a place?

01 / 05The question

The question

A welcome is a beginning, not an ending.

The longer I listened, the less a single definition of home seemed useful.

My research question is: How do international students at Texas Tech adjust to life in Lubbock, and what makes them feel they belong? I used open-ended interviews to hear how students describe this experience in their own words. I also compared those accounts with Texas Tech’s published student-support resources and research on international student belonging.

Three participants are from Nigeria; the fourth is from Ghana. Their routes to Texas Tech vary: Virginia, Canada, Dallas, and Ghana itself feature in their accounts. Two were new to the university, while others had spent longer building routines and communities. The fourth interview introduced a different view of cultural familiarity and belonging at work and church.

Four interviews cannot describe every international student’s experience. The participants differ in their histories and responses, and the comparisons below are questions to pursue, not a survey or proof about the whole campus.

02 / 05Arriving

Arrival / expectations & surprises

Not everyone arrives with the same picture.

Before moving to Lubbock, the first two participants imagined a deserted city with little to do. Participant 03 had few expectations but noticed how much quieter Lubbock felt than Dallas. Participant 04 remembered driving in from elsewhere and seeing dry, brown surroundings. For her, too, the city offered less variety than larger places such as Dallas. Four arrivals produced different impressions, but several shared a feeling that Lubbock was smaller and less busy than expected.

Participants 01 and 02 were surprised by the friendliness of people at Texas Tech: one noticed greetings, while the other was struck by a cheerful campus. Participant 03 compared the city's pace with Dallas. Participant 04 said the university itself helps make Lubbock feel more lively. This last observation separates the campus from the surrounding city—an important distinction when asking whether someone feels at home at Texas Tech or in Lubbock.

“People actually greet you.”

Interview 01 / comparing Lubbock with Virginia

These differences made me cautious about imagining a single international-student experience. Expectations are filtered through earlier homes, family, travel, and the first view of a place. Arrival can also mean entering a new culture: Participant 04 contrasted neighbors knowing one another in Ghana with her impression that people in Lubbock often keep to themselves. She described that as her own experience, not a claim about everyone here.

03 / 05Connecting

Everyday life / routines & relationships

The familiar places are not always the same places.

The first three participants independently mentioned the Texas Tech library. One liked seeing other people while studying; another goes there almost daily; the third remembered spending long study sessions there. Participant 04 chose a different everyday place: her campus workplace, where colleagues work with her schedule and listen to her concerns. She also mentioned going to the Student Union Building for food. Together, these answers show that the place that feels comfortable might be a study space, a workplace, or a quick stop for a meal—not necessarily a landmark.

Relationships are equally varied. Participant 01 described professors, classmates and campus clubs. Participant 02 mentioned church, hackathons, and library technology workshops after initially finding it difficult to make friends. Participant 03 described church, advisers and classmates she repeatedly met during her degree. Participant 04 highlighted church and the Society of Women Engineers, where conversations about jobs, interviews and projects help her connect with others.

Community close-up / Tree of Restoration (TOR)

What happens between Sunday and feeling known?

Participants 01 and 02 named Tree of Restoration (TOR). Its website describes a student-centered church community with hospitality, worship, outreach and creative teams, plus a volunteer ride ministry called Ubers For The Lord. These are services the church says it offers, not proof that either participant joined a specific team. Their interviews suggest why being greeted repeatedly and having a way to get there could help turn an unfamiliar place into a community.

Several interviews complicate the idea of a universally welcoming campus. Participant 02 noticed heated political debates; Participant 03 sometimes felt outside conversations about sports. Participant 04 described the work of forming friendships across cultures when shared references are missing. She did not say everyone excludes her; she explained how having to repeatedly translate a cultural experience can make some conversations harder.

Routines matter because they create opportunities for recognition and trust. But they are not sufficient on their own. When a friend understands your background without a lengthy explanation, or a supervisor listens to your situation, a setting can become meaningful. I learned about these spaces through interviews; the campus photograph illustrates the library but is not evidence of my own observation.

04 / 05Movement

The city beyond the campus

Belonging also depends on where you can go.

Interviews 03 and 04 both reveal a practical condition of life in Lubbock: neither participant drives—but they describe transportation differently.

Participant 03 described transportation as a constraint: without a car, deciding to visit a store or another part of town requires more planning. Participant 04 also has no car and described the time and expense involved in obtaining one. But she spoke positively about the bus services she uses to get to campus and off campus. When a bus is unavailable, an Uber costs extra, so sometimes walking is preferable. Her answer complicates an easy conclusion that transportation is simply inaccessible: the available services help, but options and money still shape decisions.

“I can't just drive myself somewhere.”

This connection between transportation and belonging matters because access to community is physical as well as social. Joining a club or reaching a workplace depends on whether the student can get there, on their schedule and within their budget. Two students without cars encounter the same city differently; their accounts depend on which routes are available and where they actually need to travel.

Texas Tech's transportation page says Citibus serves on-campus and off-campus routes, including routes students can use with their TTU ID. The university also describes Raider Ride as an on-campus evening shuttle. Such options matter, but route schedules and the location of a student's home may determine whether a trip is realistic. University pages explain services; interviews explain lived access.

Four interviews cannot show how widespread those barriers are across Texas Tech. They do show why I should ask about everyday material conditions, not just emotions. Feeling at home on campus and having freedom to explore the rest of Lubbock are separate questions worth pursuing.

05 / 05Belonging

The distinction / belonging versus home

A community can feel like home before a city does.

Participant 01 called Lubbock her third home: Nigeria first, Virginia second, and Texas Tech’s city third. She appreciates welcoming classrooms and campus groups but said her closest family and friendship ties are elsewhere. Participant 02 is more hesitant: he said he has not developed the deep connections that would make Lubbock his second home, and that he could leave without feeling particularly upset. He nevertheless spoke positively about classes, activities, and church.

Participant 03 complicates the question after several years on campus. She describes friendships and church as home-like, then thinks again about calling Lubbock her second home because Dallas remains meaningful. Participant 04 makes a related but distinct point. She does not identify the city itself as home, yet she says the people in her community make her feel welcomed and culturally understood. For these two participants, a home-like community is not necessarily identical to a home-like city.

The advice across interviews is not identical. The first participant recommends events while keeping academics in balance. The second advises new students to be proactive. The third says not to force belonging by joining the wrong group. The fourth similarly warns against forcing oneself to fit into an unfamiliar culture; instead, she recommends finding people one relates to and helping others understand one’s perspective. Their comments leave me asking whether belonging means changing yourself, finding familiar people, building new relationships, or combining all three.

Together, the four accounts show that welcome, familiarity and home do not always arrive at the same time. Students described campus friendliness, repeated study routines, supportive work relationships and religious community. They also described distance from family, difficult conversations across cultures, and limits on movement. These are different dimensions of belonging, and each matters in a different way.

These interviews cannot tell me what every international student experiences, and I have not independently observed the interactions they describe. They can, however, sharpen the research question: Is belonging located in the city, in the university, or in particular relationships? Four students gave answers that overlap without becoming identical.

THREE VOICES, NOT A UNIVERSAL RULE

Three participants are originally from Nigeria; the fourth is from Ghana. Their interviews reveal different timeframes, relationships and transport experiences, but this small group does not represent the full diversity of international students.

Sources in conversation

What the interviews add to the bigger picture.

Texas Tech's Global Guides program is designed to help incoming international students navigate a new school, city, and culture through peer guidance and events. The university's Students for Global Connections organization similarly describes events that bring international and domestic students together. These resources document opportunities that exist; my interviews explore what being welcomed feels like and how connections do—or do not—grow into lasting relationships.

A 2026 systematic review by Zhouer Li and Carla Bohndick identified different personal, institutional, and person-environment factors affecting international students' social interactions. Its emphasis on repeated contact and the surrounding environment offers a way to understand why Participant 03 talked about classmates and church she had encountered across several semesters. It does not prove her experience is typical.

A 2024 literature review by Dania Mohamad and Keenan Daniel Manning notes that “belonging” is not defined consistently in research on international students. The four interviews illustrate this problem in ordinary language: one student counted a third home; another would not call Lubbock a second; one hesitated between different places; and one located home mainly in the community rather than the city.

Tree of Restoration's ministries description adds local context to the interviews about church: hospitality volunteers greet guests and the ride ministry helps people reach Sunday services. A qualitative study of 15 international students at a Christian university also examined social support and religiousness, although it cannot predict the experiences of students in Lubbock.

The Texas Tech Student Government Association documents campus and off-campus transport options. That information does not reveal whether the service meets a person's particular schedule or budget; it helps explain why Participants 03 and 04 experienced getting around Lubbock differently.

I interviewed four international students using a shared set of questions about arrival, culture, relationships and home, with an added prompt about transportation and material conditions in the later interviews. I compared similarities and disagreements, then consulted university resources and academic literature for context.
