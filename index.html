Far from home.

Finding a place to belong does not always mean finding another home.

I spoke with an international student at Texas Tech about arriving in Lubbock, making connections and the people she still calls home. What stayed with me was not a simple story of fitting in. It was the difference between being welcomed and feeling at home.

01 / 04The question

The question

What actually makes a place feel like home?

When you move somewhere new, people often ask whether you like it. But enjoying a place and belonging to it are not necessarily the same thing.

For this project, I am exploring how international students at Texas Tech navigate the cultural, social and everyday adjustments of life in Lubbock. I want to understand not just whether students feel included on campus, but how that feeling relates to the people and places they consider home.

My first conversation was with a student originally from Nigeria. She had also lived in Virginia for about two years before coming to Texas Tech. That experience gives her three points of comparison: where she is from, where her family lives, and where she studies now.

I am keeping her unnamed in the published draft. This is one person's account; her experience is not evidence of what every international student thinks.

02 / 04Arriving

Arrival / expectations & surprises

She expected a quiet, empty place. She found people.

Before coming to Texas Tech, the student thought Lubbock might be boring and deserted. The surprise was the social atmosphere: she noticed a diverse student body and people who were willing to talk to her.

She compared this with her time in Virginia, where, in her experience, people generally seemed more focused on their own lives. In Lubbock, she said, “people actually greet you.” It was a small difference, but one she noticed right away.

That detail interests me because an unfamiliar city does not become familiar through its buildings alone. Sometimes, the first thing that changes our perception of a place is how someone treats us there. A greeting does not create a lifelong attachment, but it can make a new environment feel less closed off.

“People actually greet you.”

Interview 01 / comparing Lubbock with Virginia

Her answer also complicates the idea of cultural adjustment. She did not describe major cultural difficulties in daily life; she described a change in social behavior. I should not assume that moving from one place to another necessarily produces the same kind of culture shock for every student.

03 / 04Connecting

Everyday life / routines & relationships

Sometimes a place feels different because of who is there.

When I asked where she felt most comfortable, she mentioned the library, her classes, the gym and Texas Tech games. What links these places is not that they look alike. They are all environments where she can do something with or around other people.

The library, for example, is both somewhere to study and somewhere to see familiar signs of campus life. She also described professors as friendly and group work in class as a way of getting to know her classmates. Her account suggests that belonging can grow through ordinary routines, not just special events.

Student organizations appeared repeatedly in the conversation, too. She named TOR, the National Society of Black Engineers, the Society of Women Engineers and the African Student Organization. She spoke positively about the welcome at activities organized by the international student office and everyday clubs.

At the same time, she said she does not go off campus very much. That raises a question I have not yet answered: does a student feel connected to Texas Tech in the same way she feels connected to Lubbock? I plan to explore that distinction more carefully in future interviews.

04 / 04Belonging

The distinction / belonging versus home

Being welcomed is real. So is missing the people who know you.

The interview's most important moment, to me, came when I asked whether she would consider Texas Tech or Lubbock her second home. She instead described it as her third home. Nigeria was first. Virginia, where her family lives, was second.

That answer does not cancel her positive experiences at Texas Tech. She had already told me about welcoming clubs, approachable professors and places on campus where she felt comfortable. But those experiences do not automatically replace the people and relationships associated with her earlier homes.

Here is the distinction I am beginning to explore: welcoming spaces can help someone participate in a community, while the feeling of home may depend on relationships built over much longer periods of time. This is a tentative interpretation of one interview, not a conclusion about all international students.

She said she does not have family or close friends in Lubbock, and she also mentioned missing her family and finding fewer things to do in the city than in the places she had lived before. The adjustment she described was therefore both social and emotional. A university can provide activity and connection without removing the distance between a student and people they love.

“It's my third home, because my first home is Nigeria and my second home is Virginia…”

Interview 01 / interviewer-updated transcript

Her advice to a student who is struggling to settle in was to attend events and meet people, while still protecting time for academics. That is useful advice because it comes out of what she experienced herself. But I also want to hear from someone whose experiences have been different—someone for whom campus activities did not lead to connection, or someone who began calling Lubbock home very quickly.

WHAT ONE INTERVIEW CANNOT ANSWER

Do on-campus and off-campus students experience belonging differently? How do transportation, employment, cost and housing affect the picture? These questions will guide the next stage.

Interviewer / 01

Where are you originally from, and how long have you been studying at Texas Tech University?

Participant

I'm originally from Nigeria, but then I stayed in Virginia for about two years before moving to Texas. I've been studying at Texas Tech for two month now.

Interviewer / 02

Before coming to Texas Tech, what did you expect life in Lubbock to be like?

Participant

I thought it was going to be boring, deserted, and, yeah, extra boring.

Interviewer / 03

What surprised you most when you first arrived at Texas Tech?

Participant

The community. Like, a lot of kind people, and it has a very good diversity rate. So there's always a place for everybody. And yeah, there are actually people here.

Interviewer / 04

What has been the most difficult part of adjusting to life in Lubbock?

Participant

When I miss my family, and there's not so much to do. Like, I mean, there is, but compared to where I'm coming from, there isn't so much to do.

Interviewer / 05

Have you experienced any cultural differences that affected your everyday life?

Participant

Not exactly. It's just like the people here are more friendly. I'm used to people being so focused on their lives in Virginia. So when I came here, people actually greet you. It was kind of different, but not life-changing different.

Interviewer / 06

Can you describe a time when you felt particularly welcomed or excluded at Texas Tech?

Participant

Welcomed? Like, you always feel welcomed at all the events. From the International Student Office to the normal everyday clubs, there will always be somebody to come greet you, come talk to you in every single activity. So I've never felt excluded from any events.

Interviewer / 07

What people, activities, or organizations have helped you feel connected to the Texas Tech community?

Participant

There's TOR, the National Society of Black Engineers, the Society of Women Engineers, and the African Student Organization.

Interviewer / follow-up

What about people?

Participant

My professors are pretty friendly. Then my classmates, they also get us to work in teams, so that helps you know more people. And yeah, the people here are generally kind.

Interviewer / 08

Are there particular places on campus or around Lubbock where you feel most comfortable? What makes those places special?

Participant

I like the library 'cause you get to see a lot of people, and it's nice to study. Then, to be honest, my classes actually feel comfortable and nice for me. I don't really go off campus, so no answer to lubbock in general. Oh yeah, and the gym. The gym also makes me feel very comfortable and welcome. And then the Texas Tech games that I've been to, they're also really fun.

Interviewer / 09

Would you consider Lubbock or Texas Tech your second home? Why or why not?

Participant

I think consider It's my third home, because my first home is Nigeria and my second home is Virginia, because that's where my family members are. I don't really have family or close friends in Lubbock. That's just why I feel like the people there make a home, and the people that are making home for me are not here, so.

Interviewer / 10

Looking back on your experience, what advice would you give to a new international student struggling to feel at home?

Participant

I don't think they should have problems, 'cause people will always talk to you. you can make friends at international student events. So yeah, I think it's important to go to events, not so many that you're distracted from your academics, but you should definitely go. There will always be people there willing to welcome you and stuff. Yeah.

Interviewer / closing

Okay, thank you very much for your time. I really appreciate it.
