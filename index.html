Far from home.

Two students found a welcoming campus. Neither gave a simple answer about home.

I asked two international students what life in Lubbock has been like since they arrived. They both expected a quiet, nearly deserted place. Instead, they described friendly people, familiar routines, and student communities. But when I asked whether this was home, their answers made me think about the distance between being welcome and belonging.

01 / 04The question

The question

A friendly place is not automatically home.

What surprised me was how much two conversations had in common—and how differently each person answered the question of home.

For this project, I am asking how international students at Texas Tech adjust to Lubbock and what helps—or prevents—them from feeling they belong. Interviews let me hear the difference between campus life as an institution and the personal relationships that make somewhere feel like home.

One participant came from Nigeria after living in Virginia for about two years. Another also came from Nigeria but had spent several years in Canada. Both had been at Texas Tech for only a few months when I spoke with them.

I am presenting these as two individual experiences. I am not identifying the participants on this page, and I am not using two interviews to make claims about every international student.

“I was surprised by the community.”

02 / 04Arriving

Arrival / expectations & surprises

They both expected an empty city.

Before moving to Lubbock, both students imagined somewhere deserted, with little to do. The first expected it to be "extra boring." The second also expected a place without many activities. Neither account began with excitement about the city.

What changed those expectations was not the skyline or the weather. Both talked about people. The first participant was struck by how easily others greeted her, especially compared with her experience in Virginia. The second spoke about the friendliness and cheerfulness of people on campus; his opinion of Texas Tech itself also improved after he arrived.

“People actually greet you.”

Interview 01 / comparing Lubbock with Virginia

I find this comparison interesting because both students were imagining a place before they really knew it. Their early experiences changed those assumptions. But feeling pleasantly surprised is still different from having deep roots somewhere.

03 / 04Connecting

Everyday life / routines & relationships

The library came up in both conversations.

It surprised me that both students mentioned the same ordinary place: the Texas Tech library. For the first student, it is somewhere she can study and see other people. The second goes there almost every day and describes it as the place where he studies most often.

Their other routes into the community are different. The first student talked about welcoming events, classes, professors, teamwork, the gym, games and student organizations including TOR, the National Society of Black Engineers, the Society of Women Engineers and the African Student Organization. The second mentioned hackathons, technology workshops at the library, church, and engineering-related events. He also said that making friends was difficult at first, even though he now finds it easier.

FROM INTERVIEW 01

“My professors are pretty friendly. Then my classmates, they also get us to work in teams, so that helps you know more people.”

Participant 01 / how classmates and professors create connections

The second student described something I had not heard in the first interview: how heated political disagreement can become part of people's identities. He did not say it significantly changed his everyday routine, but he noticed this cultural difference.

These details make the word community less abstract. It can mean attending an event, recognizing the library as your regular study space, joining a conversation after class, or returning to the same church. I need to be careful, though: familiar routines do not tell me everything about how supported a person feels.

The part I keep returning to

What counts as home?

The first student named three homes: Nigeria, Virginia and Lubbock. Explore what each place means in her answer. The second student did not yet consider Lubbock a second home; his family lives in Houston.

FIRST HOME / COUNTRY OF ORIGIN

Nigeria

She identifies Nigeria as her first home. The interview does not explore its specific places and traditions in depth, so this part of her story is still open for further questions.

What this tells me: a place's importance cannot always be measured by how long someone currently spends there.

THE SECOND RESPONSE

Participant 02 said that for now, Lubbock was not really his second home. He described enjoying his time at Texas Tech, but he also spoke about family in Houston and wanting to be near them. This difference is why I should not assign the same meaning of “home” to both students.

04 / 04Belonging

The distinction / belonging versus home

Welcoming people cannot replace every relationship.

When I asked about a second home, the first student called Lubbock her third. Nigeria was first; Virginia, where her family lives, was second. She described friendships and welcoming campus spaces in Texas but also acknowledged that she does not have family or close friends in Lubbock.

The second student answered differently. He said he has not yet formed the deep connections that would make Lubbock his second home. In fact, he said he could leave the next day without feeling particularly bad. His family is in Houston, and he loves being around them. He also made clear that he enjoys living here and values the friendliness, church, events and other opportunities at Texas Tech.

“It's my third home, because my first home is Nigeria and my second home is Virginia…”

FROM INTERVIEW 02

“For now, not really, because I haven't developed deep connections to say it is a second home.”

Participant 02 / interviewer-updated transcript

To me, the two answers point to a distinction that a simple yes-or-no question misses. Students can appreciate and participate in a place without ranking it as one of their deepest homes. Both have meaningful family ties somewhere else, but neither interview suggests that campus friendships are unimportant.

The advice the students offered was also similar. One recommended student events without neglecting academics. The other urged new students to be proactive, avoid isolating themselves and make friends. I hear optimism in both answers, even though neither describes Lubbock as an uncomplicated replacement for places and people they already love.

My next research step is to document campus places myself and speak with students who have lived here longer. Two interviews can reveal interesting questions; they cannot settle the experience of an entire community.

A LIMIT OF THESE INTERVIEWS

Both students are relatively new to Texas Tech, and both have family elsewhere. Longer-term students, different housing situations and off-campus experiences could complicate this picture.

Interview 01 transcript updated by the interviewer after reviewing the captions. Not time-aligned.

Interviewer / 01

Where are you originally from, and how long have you been studying at Texas Tech University?

Participant

I'm originally from Nigeria, but then I stayed in Virginia for about two years before moving to Texas. I've been studying at Texas Tech for two month now.

Interviewer / 02

Before coming to Texas Tech, what did you expect life in Lubbock to be like?

Participant

I thought it was going to be boring, deserted, and, yeah, extra boring.

Interviewer / 03

What surprised you most when you first arrived at Texas Tech?

Participant

The community. Like, a lot of kind people, and it has a very good diversity rate. So there's always a place for everybody. And yeah, there are actually people here.

Interviewer / 04

What has been the most difficult part of adjusting to life in Lubbock?

Participant

When I miss my family, and there's not so much to do. Like, I mean, there is, but compared to where I'm coming from, there isn't so much to do.

Interviewer / 05

Have you experienced any cultural differences that affected your everyday life?

Participant

Not exactly. It's just like the people here are more friendly. I'm used to people being so focused on their lives in Virginia. So when I came here, people actually greet you. It was kind of different, but not life-changing different.

Interviewer / 06

Can you describe a time when you felt particularly welcomed or excluded at Texas Tech?

Participant

Welcomed? Like, you always feel welcomed at all the events. From the International Student Office to the normal everyday clubs, there will always be somebody to come greet you, come talk to you in every single activity. So I've never felt excluded from any events.

Interviewer / 07

What people, activities, or organizations have helped you feel connected to the Texas Tech community?

Participant

There's TOR, the National Society of Black Engineers, the Society of Women Engineers, and the African Student Organization.

Interviewer / follow-up

What about people?

Participant

My professors are pretty friendly. Then my classmates, they also get us to work in teams, so that helps you know more people. And yeah, the people here are generally kind.

Interviewer / 08

Are there particular places on campus or around Lubbock where you feel most comfortable? What makes those places special?

Participant

I like the library 'cause you get to see a lot of people, and it's nice to study. Then, to be honest, my classes actually feel comfortable and nice for me. I don't really go off campus, so no answer to Lubbock in general. Oh yeah, and the gym. The gym also makes me feel very comfortable and welcome. And then the Texas Tech games that I've been to, they're also really fun.

Interviewer / 09

Would you consider Lubbock or Texas Tech your second home? Why or why not?

Participant

I think consider It's my third home, because my first home is Nigeria and my second home is Virginia, because that's where my family members are. I don't really have family or close friends in Lubbock. That's just why I feel like the people there make a home, and the people that are making home for me are not here, so.

Interviewer / 10

Looking back on your experience, what advice would you give to a new international student struggling to feel at home?

Participant

I don't think they should have problems, 'cause people will always talk to you. You can make friends at international student events. So yeah, I think it's important to go to events, not so many that you're distracted from your academics, but you should definitely go. There will always be people there willing to welcome you and stuff. Yeah.

Interviewer / closing

Okay, thank you very much for your time. I really appreciate it.

Read interview 02 · Not quite home10 questions ↓

Updated by the interviewer from the recorded conversation. Punctuation and grammar have been lightly edited; this is not a time-aligned verbatim transcript.

Interviewer / 01

Where are you originally from, and how long have you been studying at Texas Tech University?

Participant 02

I'm originally from Nigeria, but I've also stayed in Canada for a few years. I've been studying at Texas Tech University for about three months.

Interviewer / 02

Before coming to Texas Tech, what did you expect life in Lubbock to be like?

Participant 02

I thought Lubbock was like a deserted area. It had nothing. There were not a lot of things to do. It wasn't a fun place to be. That's really what I thought about Lubbock in general.

Interviewer / 03

What surprised you most when you first arrived at Texas Tech?

Participant 02

I was surprised by the community at Texas Tech University: how friendly everyone is, how cheerful everyone is, and also how good the university is. Initially I thought the university wasn't very good, but now my opinion about it has changed.

Interviewer / 04

What has been the most difficult part of adjusting to life in Lubbock?

Participant 02

Initially it was hard for me to make friends, talk to people. But now I've gotten better at it, and it's been going great. The people have been very welcoming and good to me.

Interviewer / 05

Have you experienced any cultural differences that affected your everyday life?

Participant 02

I wouldn't say it has affected my everyday life. A cultural difference is how people take political differences as an identity. You can see serious and heated debates around politics, which is something I wasn't used to. I don't really take it to heart. I don't really make it my identity.

Interviewer / 06

Can you describe a time when you felt particularly welcomed or excluded at Texas Tech?

Participant 02

I don't think I've ever felt particularly excluded at any event on Texas Tech. I've felt welcomed at various events, including my classes, hackathons that I've attended, and even the church that I go to, TOR. It has been very welcoming for me.

Interviewer / 07

What people, activities, or organizations have helped you feel connected to the Texas Tech community?

Participant 02

I haven't really joined any club activities for now, but some of the things that have made me feel connected to the Texas Tech community are probably the National Society of Black Engineers, the various events that I go to, the library workshops where I learn new things about technology, and my church. Our church has made me feel connected to Lubbock and Texas Tech in general.

Interviewer / 08

Are there particular places on campus or around Lubbock where you feel most comfortable? What makes those places special?

Participant 02

I'd say probably the library, because that's where I go every day. That's where I study most times. I don't really go out that often. The library is a very good place for me.

Interviewer / 09

Would you consider Lubbock or Texas Tech your second home? Why or why not?

Participant 02

For now, not really, because I haven't developed deep connections to say it is a second home. If I were to leave the next day i wouldn't feel bad. That doesn't mean that I haven't enjoyed every single time I've been here. But to call it my second home, I wouldn't really say so, because I do have family back in Houston, and I really love to be around them.

Interviewer / 10

Looking back on your experience, what advice would you give to a new international student struggling to feel at home?

Participant 02

I'd say be proactive. Everyone here is welcoming; everyone here is cheerful. Always attend activities. It's not good to be alone on your own. Make friends.
