Far from home.

Three international students. Three different ways of finding home in Lubbock.

I spoke with three students about coming to Texas Tech, making friends, finding places to return to, and deciding whether Lubbock feels like home. Two arrived expecting an empty city; the third came without many expectations at all. Their stories made me rethink a simple question: Is feeling welcomed the same as belonging?

01 / 05The question

The question

A welcome is a beginning, not an ending.

Listening to students at different stages of university life changed the way I understand the word home.

My research question is: How do international students at Texas Tech adjust to life in Lubbock, and what makes them feel they belong? I used open-ended interviews to hear how students describe this experience in their own words. I also compared those accounts with Texas Tech’s published student-support resources and research on international student belonging.

All three participants are originally from Nigeria, but their routes to Texas Tech differ. Participant 01 previously lived in Virginia and had been at Tech about two months. Participant 02 spent several years in Canada and had attended Tech for about three months. Participant 03 had reached her seventh semester at Tech, after coming from Dallas. That difference in time on campus revealed something the first two conversations alone could not show.

I have kept participants unnamed in the published text. Three interviews cannot describe every international student’s experience; they can show how a shared campus produces different attachments, routines, and obstacles.

02 / 05Arriving

Arrival / expectations & surprises

Not everyone arrives with the same picture.

The first two participants imagined Lubbock as a deserted place with little to do. Interview 01 described expecting it to be “extra boring,” while Interview 02 pictured a city without many activities. The third student complicates that neat pattern: she said she had hardly formed an image of Lubbock before visiting for orientation. Her initial surprise was the city’s quieter pace compared with Dallas—not a failed prediction about a place she had imagined in detail.

Participants 01 and 02 found the friendliness of people at Tech more striking than they expected. One noticed everyday greetings; the other said the campus community was cheerful and the university better than expected. The more experienced third participant remembered noticing how unlike Dallas the city looked and felt. These accounts remind me that “first impressions” depend on what students bring with them: family locations, past cities, expectations, and even whether they arrive with a clear image of Lubbock.

“People actually greet you.”

Interview 01 / comparing Lubbock with Virginia

Comparing their arrivals made me more cautious about treating “international students” as one category. Two people can agree about campus friendliness but differ in what they expected, and a third can arrive without the same anxieties. What people notice first is not necessarily what matters most years later.

03 / 05Connecting

Everyday life / routines & relationships

The library is the thread connecting all three stories.

All three participants independently named the Texas Tech library as a comfortable place. The first liked being around people while studying. The second described going there nearly every day. The third, who has spent years at Tech, said she used to spend almost half her day studying there. The same building appeared in three accounts, but it meant something slightly different each time: people nearby, dependable routine, or the memory of time spent working.

The surrounding relationships differ. Participant 01 described warm interactions at student events and with professors, classmates, and groups such as the National Society of Black Engineers, the Society of Women Engineers, and the African Student Organization. Participant 02 mentioned church, hackathons, engineering events and library workshops; making friends was initially difficult. Participant 03 repeatedly returned to church, classmates in her biochemistry major, and the support of professors and advisers. Over several semesters, she had begun seeing the same classmates again and again. For her, connection was less about one welcome event than a network built through repetition.

FROM INTERVIEW 01

“My professors are pretty friendly. Then my classmates, they also get us to work in teams, so that helps you know more people.”

Participant 01 / how classmates and professors create connections

The second participant also noticed the intensity of political discussions, although he did not say it shaped his everyday life. The third participant mentioned a different small exclusion: feeling lost when conversations turned to sports, because she does not follow them. Neither episode should be generalized into a campus-wide pattern, but they show that students can be included in one setting and feel outside a conversation in another.

Routines are significant, but they are not the whole story. What emerges across the interviews is that familiar places become meaningful through what people do there and who recognizes them. That insight also suggests a limit to my method: I heard students describe the library, but I did not personally observe the conversations or relationships they experienced there. The archival library photo illustrates the location; it is not fieldwork evidence.

04 / 05Movement

The city beyond the campus

Belonging also depends on where you can go.

Interview 03 brought up a practical limit that the other conversations barely explored: she does not drive.

When I asked about the hardest part of living in Lubbock, Participant 03 talked about learning to navigate the city. Later, I asked a new question about everyday conditions such as housing, transportation, finances, and access to activities. Transportation stood out again. Going to a store like Walmart or visiting another part of town is not always something she can decide to do on her own. She described her finances as manageable because she works, while most of the organizations she attends meet on campus and fit her schedule.

FROM INTERVIEW 03 / ABOUT TRANSPORTATION

“I can't just drive myself somewhere.”

Edited from Interview 03 captions · 05:52–06:03

This changes how I think about the research question. A student might feel comfortable in classrooms, libraries, and church but still have limited freedom to experience Lubbock beyond those places. Community can depend on access. If participation requires traveling across town, the ability to drive, find transportation, or rely on someone else can matter as much as whether an event sounds welcoming.

My three interviews do not establish how widespread transportation difficulties are. They do reveal a distinction worth keeping: belonging at Texas Tech is not identical to belonging in the city of Lubbock. The physical ability to move between places affects how students encounter that city.

WHAT CHANGED IN MY RESEARCH

The additional question about transportation and finances produced new evidence. Without it, this project would have focused almost entirely on emotions and friendships.

05 / 05Belonging

The distinction / belonging versus home

No single definition of home fits these stories.

Participant 01 called Lubbock her third home: Nigeria first, Virginia second, and Texas Tech’s city third. She appreciates welcoming classrooms and campus groups but said her closest family and friendship ties are elsewhere. Participant 02 is more hesitant: he said he has not developed the deep connections that would make Lubbock his second home, and that he could leave without feeling particularly upset. He nevertheless spoke positively about classes, activities, and church.

Participant 03 gives the most complicated answer. She has spent several years at Tech, built friendships and a church community, and said Lubbock could be a second home because of those people. Then she reconsidered that label, mentioning Dallas as another home. Rather than force her answer into a yes or no, I hear an important distinction: having people who make a place meaningful does not necessarily settle how someone ranks the places that matter to them.

“It's my third home, because my first home is Nigeria and my second home is Virginia…”

Interview 01 / interviewer-corrected transcript

The advice in the interviews reflects their circumstances. The first participant encouraged attending events without neglecting schoolwork. The second urged new students to be proactive and make friends. The third gave a different warning: “Don’t try to fit in.” She advised students not to rush into a group simply to belong and said the friendships made in a first year may change over time. Her longer experience gives that advice a different weight.

Taken together, I cannot conclude that Texas Tech automatically makes students feel at home. I can say that these students described multiple kinds of connection: cheerful everyday greetings, repeated study routines, friendships built over semesters, and religious community. Just as important, they showed what these connections do not always solve: missing family, lacking deep roots, or finding it difficult to move around the city. Those contrasts are the heart of this inquiry.

This project suggests a way to keep asking the question rather than closing it: Which relationships last? Which spaces become familiar? And how do everyday constraints shape what students experience beyond campus? The third interview reminded me that belonging has a history; it changes as people spend time in a place, find some communities, and leave others behind.

THREE VOICES, NOT A UNIVERSAL RULE

All three participants are originally from Nigeria, so national background is not varied in this sample. Their contrasting time at Tech and their family connections still show how different meanings of home can emerge.

Interviewer / 01

Where are you originally from, and how long have you been studying at Texas Tech University?

Participant 02

Before coming to Texas Tech, what did you expect life in Lubbock to be like?

Participant 02

I thought Lubbock was like a deserted area. It had nothing. There were not a lot of things to do. It wasn't a fun place to be. That's really what I thought about Lubbock in general.

Interviewer / 03

What surprised you most when you first arrived at Texas Tech?

Participant 02

I was surprised by the community at Texas Tech University: how friendly everyone is, how cheerful everyone is, and also how good the university is. Initially I thought the university wasn't very good, but now my opinion about it has changed.

Interviewer / 04

What has been the most difficult part of adjusting to life in Lubbock?

Participant 02

Initially it was hard for me to make friends, talk to people. But now I've gotten better at it, and it's been going great. The people have been very welcoming and good to me.

Interviewer / 05

Have you experienced any cultural differences that affected your everyday life?

Participant 02

I wouldn't say it has affected my everyday life. A cultural difference is how people take political differences as an identity. You can see serious and heated debates around politics, which is something I wasn't used to. I don't really take it to heart. I don't really make it my identity.

Interviewer / 06

Can you describe a time when you felt particularly welcomed or excluded at Texas Tech?

Participant 02

I don't think I've ever felt particularly excluded at any event on Texas Tech. I've felt welcomed at various events, including my classes, hackathons that I've attended, and even the church that I go to, TOR. It has been very welcoming for me.

Interviewer / 07

What people, activities, or organizations have helped you feel connected to the Texas Tech community?

Participant 02

I haven't really joined any club activities for now, but some of the things that have made me feel connected to the Texas Tech community are probably the National Society of Black Engineers, the various events that I go to, the library workshops where I learn new things about technology, and my church. Our church has made me feel connected to Lubbock and Texas Tech in general.

Interviewer / 08

Are there particular places on campus or around Lubbock where you feel most comfortable? What makes those places special?

Participant 02

I'd say probably the library, because that's where I go every day. That's where I study most times. I don't really go out that often. The library is a very good place for me.

Interviewer / 09

Would you consider Lubbock or Texas Tech your second home? Why or why not?

Participant 02

For now, not really, because I haven't developed deep connections to say it is a second home. If I were to leave the next day I wouldn't feel bad. That doesn't mean that I haven't enjoyed every single time I've been here. But to call it my second home, I wouldn't really say so, because I do have family back in Houston, and I really love to be around them.

Interviewer / 10

Looking back on your experience, what advice would you give to a new international student struggling to feel at home?

Participant 02

I'd say be proactive. Everyone here is welcoming; everyone here is cheerful. Always attend activities. It's not good to be alone on your own. Make friends.

Where are you originally from, and how long have you been studying at Texas Tech University?

Really, nothing. I didn't give much thought to what life in Lubbock would be like. I hadn't been here before orientation, so I didn't really have a preconceived notion of it.

Interviewer / 03

What surprised you most when you first arrived at Texas Tech?

Participant 03

How the city looked and felt compared with Dallas, where I had lived. Lubbock felt much quieter, with less of a big-city atmosphere. That difference was a shock in a way.

Interviewer / 04

What has been the most difficult part of adjusting to life in Lubbock?

Participant 03

For me, driving and navigating the city. I generally don't drive, and I don't know Lubbock very well. Figuring out where to go and how to get there has been difficult.

Interviewer / 05

Have you experienced any cultural differences that affected your everyday life?

Participant 03

I don't think so. I haven't experienced any differences that affected my everyday life in that way.

Interviewer / 06

Can you describe a time when you felt particularly welcomed or excluded at Texas Tech?

Participant 03

I feel especially welcomed at my church. When I came here freshman year I didn't have a church, and I tried different places before finding this community. I haven't really felt excluded, though conversations about sports can leave me out because I'm not a sports person.

Interviewer / 07

What people, activities, or organizations have helped you feel connected to the Texas Tech community?

Participant 03

My church, TOR, people in my biochemistry major, and professors and advisers. After a while you have classes with the same people over and over, and those connections build. My professors and advisers have been very helpful.

Interviewer / 08

Are there particular places on campus or around Lubbock where you feel most comfortable?

Participant 03

On campus, mostly the library. There was a time when I studied there for almost half of my day. I also mentioned a STEM-related space [name unclear in captions]. Around Lubbock, probably places where I go out to eat.

Interviewer / 09

Would you consider Lubbock or Texas Tech your second home? Why or why not?

Participant 03

In some ways I would because many of my friends and a community I like are here. But I wouldn't necessarily call it my second home when I think about Dallas and my first home. Here I have a community, which is what makes Lubbock feel like home in a certain way. [The answer shifts between these meanings of 'second home'.]

Interviewer / 10

Looking back on your experience, what advice would you give to a new international student struggling to feel at home?

Participant 03

Don't try to fit in just for the sake of fitting in, because you might join the wrong group. Find your own community and don't let people rush you to find it immediately. People you meet in your first year may not be the same friends you have throughout college. Take your time.

Interviewer / 11

How have everyday circumstances such as housing, transportation, finances, or access to activities affected your experience of living in Lubbock?

Participant 03

Transportation is the biggest factor because I don't drive. If I want to go to Walmart or another place, I can't simply decide to drive there myself. Finances are manageable because I work. Most of my activities are through organizations on campus that fit my schedule, so access to those activities is generally okay.

Sources in conversation

What the interviews add to the bigger picture.

Texas Tech's Global Guides program is designed to help incoming international students navigate a new school, city, and culture through peer guidance and events. The university's Students for Global Connections organization similarly describes events that bring international and domestic students together. These resources document opportunities that exist; my interviews explore what being welcomed feels like and how connections do—or do not—grow into lasting relationships.

A 2026 systematic review by Zhouer Li and Carla Bohndick identified different personal, institutional, and person-environment factors affecting international students' social interactions. Its emphasis on repeated contact and the surrounding environment offers a way to understand why Participant 03 talked about classmates and church she had encountered across several semesters. It does not prove her experience is typical.

A 2024 literature review by Dania Mohamad and Keenan Daniel Manning notes that “belonging” is not defined consistently in scholarship about international students. I heard something similar in everyday language: one student called Lubbock a third home, another was not ready to call it a second, and a third could describe it as home in one sense but not another.
