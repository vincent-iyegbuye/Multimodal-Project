Far from home.

Four international students. Four ways to build a community without leaving other homes behind.

I listened to four international students describe their first impressions of Lubbock, the people they trust, and the places they return to. Their stories do not agree on what counts as home. Some value the university but miss family elsewhere; another finds home in relationships more than in the city itself. That distinction leads this investigation: Can someone feel at home with people without feeling at home in a place?

01 / 05The question

The question

A welcome is a beginning, not an ending.

The longer I listened, the less a single definition of home seemed useful.

I began with a question: how do international students at Texas Tech adjust to Lubbock, and what makes this place feel like home? I asked four students about arrival, friendships, daily routines and belonging, then compared their answers with campus resources and published research.

Three participants are from Nigeria and one is from Ghana. They arrived by different routes, including time in Virginia, Canada and Dallas. Some were newcomers; others had years to build routines here.

These are four personal accounts, not a survey of the entire international-student community. I want to understand the differences, not flatten them into one answer.

A note from the interviewer / 01

At first I thought the question about a “second home” would get a straightforward yes or no. Instead, one student called Lubbock her third home, and another said he had not formed close enough connections yet. I realized I was asking them to fit their lives into a label that might not fit.

“Don’t try to fit in.”

02 / 05Arriving

Arrival / expectations & surprises

Not everyone arrives with the same picture.

Participants 01 and 02 expected a nearly deserted city. Participant 03 noticed how much quieter Lubbock felt than Dallas, while Participant 04 remembered the dry, brown landscape she saw on the drive in. Their first impressions depended partly on the places they knew before Texas.

The first student noticed strangers greeting her; the second was struck by a cheerful campus. Participant 04 said TTU itself makes Lubbock feel livelier. Their answers made me separate a welcoming university from the larger city around it.

“People actually greet you.”

Interview 01 / comparing Lubbock with Virginia

Participant 04 also compared the neighborly familiarity she knew in Ghana with people she encountered in Lubbock, whom she perceived as more private. It was her personal impression, not a judgment about everyone in either place.

FOUR FIRST IMPRESSIONS

Two anticipated a nearly empty city; one had few expectations; one was struck by the landscape and the university’s role in making the city active.

03 / 05Connecting

Everyday life / routines & relationships

The familiar places are not always the same places.

Three participants talked about the TTU library: seeing people while studying, visiting almost daily, or spending long hours working there. Participant 04 felt comfortable at her campus job, where colleagues accommodated her schedule, and also mentioned the Student Union. Their answers brought an abstract word like “belonging” down to ordinary places.

The places mattered, but so did the people in them. One participant praised supportive professors and classmates; another mentioned hackathons, church and technology workshops. The longer-term students described advisers, coworkers, church friends and the Society of Women Engineers. The kinds of support differed, even when each person felt welcomed.

Community close-up / Tree of Restoration (TOR)

What happens between Sunday and feeling known?

Participants 01 and 02 named Tree of Restoration (TOR). TOR describes teams for hospitality, worship and creative media, as well as a volunteer ride ministry. That is what the organization offers; the interviews do not establish that either student participated in those particular teams. Still, these activities show what practical welcome might look like beyond campus.

Welcome was not the whole story. Participant 02 noticed heated political conversations; Participant 03 sometimes felt outside sports discussions; and Participant 04 explained how missing shared cultural references could make friendships harder. She was describing a challenge, not saying everyone excluded her.

A familiar place offers repeated chances to meet people. But recognition does not always become a close friendship, and a supportive supervisor is not the same as family. These are interpretations of what participants reported, not scenes I personally observed.

04 / 05Movement

The city beyond the campus

Belonging also depends on where you can go.

Interviews 03 and 04 both reveal a practical condition of life in Lubbock: neither participant drives—but they describe transportation differently.

Participant 03 said she has to plan outings because she cannot drive. Participant 04 also has no car, but described buses she can use for trips to campus and elsewhere. If a bus is unavailable, Uber costs money and walking may be preferable. Transportation was a constraint for one student and a mixed experience for the other.

A note from the interviewer / 02

Transportation was not where I expected this project to go. Listening to Esther and Hilary made me realize that asking “Where do you feel comfortable?” is different from asking “Where can you actually get to?” I didn't ask everyone the same transport follow-up, so I can't pretend their answers are directly comparable.

Texas Tech describes Citibus routes students can ride with a TTU ID and its evening on-campus Raider Ride service. Those resources matter, but published routes cannot tell me whether an individual student’s destination or schedule is covered.

I cannot tell from four interviews how common these problems are. I can say that belonging may be affected by mobility as well as social connection—and I would ask all four participants more about that if I repeated the interviews.

WHAT CHANGED IN MY RESEARCH

The follow-up question about transportation added two different experiences of living without a car: one focused on constraints, the other on helpful buses and the cost of alternatives.

05 / 05Belonging

The distinction / belonging versus home

A community can feel like home before a city does.

Participant 01 calls Lubbock her third home after Nigeria and Virginia, where family lives. Participant 02 enjoys TTU but says he has not built the deep connections needed to call Lubbock a second home. Both described welcome without treating it as a substitute for close relationships elsewhere.

After several years, Participant 03 feels at home with friends and church but still weighs what Dallas means to her. Participant 04 makes the distinction explicit: a community can feel like home when the city itself does not. Time in a place does not automatically settle the question.

“It's my third home, because my first home is Nigeria and my second home is Virginia…”

Their advice differed. One recommended events balanced with academics; another urged students to be proactive. The longer-term participants cautioned against forcing friendships or changing yourself simply to fit in. I hear a tension between reaching out and waiting for genuine connection.

Welcome, familiarity and home seem to develop at different speeds. Family distance, cultural misunderstandings and transport affect that process, even alongside positive experiences at work, school and church.

I cannot verify every interaction the students described, and four interviews cannot speak for a whole campus. Their differences leave me with a more useful question: does belonging live in a place, in people, or somewhere between them?

A note from the interviewer / 03

What stayed with me most was that the students were not simply choosing between “home” and “not home.” They were talking about different kinds of attachment—some to family, some to church, some to a workplace or study space. That feels closer to the complicated question I wanted to ask in the first place.

FOUR VOICES, NOT A UNIVERSAL RULE

Three participants are originally from Nigeria; the fourth is from Ghana. Their interviews reveal different timeframes, relationships and transport experiences, but this small group does not represent the full diversity of international students.

I thought Lubbock was like a deserted area. It had nothing. There were not a lot of things to do. It wasn't a fun place to be. That's really what I thought about Lubbock in general.

Interviewer / 03

What surprised you most when you first arrived at Texas Tech?

Participant 02

I was surprised by the community at Texas Tech University: how friendly everyone is, how cheerful everyone is, and also how good the university is. Initially I thought the university wasn't very good, but now my opinion about it has changed.

Interviewer / 04

What has been the most difficult part of adjusting to life in Lubbock?

Participant 02

Initially it was hard for me to make friends, talk to people. But now I've gotten better at it, and it's been going great. The people have been very welcoming and good to me.

Interviewer / 05

Have you experienced any cultural differences that affected your everyday life?

Participant 02

I wouldn't say it has affected my everyday life. A cultural difference is how people take political differences as an identity. You can see serious and heated debates around politics, which is something I wasn't used to. I don't really take it to heart. I don't really make it my identity.

Interviewer / 06

Can you describe a time when you felt particularly welcomed or excluded at Texas Tech?

Participant 02

I don't think I've ever felt particularly excluded at any event on Texas Tech. I've felt welcomed at various events, including my classes, hackathons that I've attended, and even the church that I go to, TOR. It has been very welcoming for me.

Interviewer / 07

What people, activities, or organizations have helped you feel connected to the Texas Tech community?

Participant 02

I haven't really joined any club activities for now, but some of the things that have made me feel connected to the Texas Tech community are probably the National Society of Black Engineers, the various events that I go to, the library workshops where I learn new things about technology, and my church. Our church has made me feel connected to Lubbock and Texas Tech in general.

Interviewer / 08

Are there particular places on campus or around Lubbock where you feel most comfortable? What makes those places special?

Participant 02

I'd say probably the library, because that's where I go every day. That's where I study most times. I don't really go out that often. The library is a very good place for me.

Interviewer / 09

Would you consider Lubbock or Texas Tech your second home? Why or why not?

Participant 02

For now, not really, because I haven't developed deep connections to say it is a second home. If I were to leave the next day I wouldn't feel bad. That doesn't mean that I haven't enjoyed every single time I've been here. But to call it my second home, I wouldn't really say so, because I do have family back in Houston, and I really love to be around them.

Interviewer / 10

Looking back on your experience, what advice would you give to a new international student struggling to feel at home?

Participant 02

I'd say be proactive. Everyone here is welcoming; everyone here is cheerful. Always attend activities. It's not good to be alone on your own. Make friends.

What people, activities, or organizations have helped you feel connected to the Texas Tech community?

My church, TOR, people in my biochemistry major, and professors and advisers. After a while you have classes with the same people over and over, and those connections build. My professors and advisers have been very helpful.

Are there particular places on campus or around Lubbock where you feel most comfortable?

On campus, mostly the library. There was a time when I studied there for almost half of my day. I also mentioned a STEM-related space [name unclear in captions]. Around Lubbock, probably places where I go out to eat.

Sources in conversation

What the interviews add to the bigger picture.

Texas Tech’s Global Guides and Students for Global Connections describe ways newcomers can meet others. They show what the university offers; my interviews show how students describe their own connections.

Li and Bohndick’s 2026 review connects students’ social interactions with institutional settings and individual circumstances. It provides context, not proof that any one participant’s experience is typical.

A 2024 review by Mohamad and Manning points out that scholars define belonging in different ways. My four interviews make those differences tangible.

TOR describes hospitality and volunteer rides. Research on religious community and support offers another perspective, but findings from other campuses cannot be assumed to describe TTU.

TTU transportation pages list services; the participants explain how mobility actually fits into daily routines. These two types of evidence answer different questions.

Research method / what I can and cannot conclude

How this story was made.

I interviewed four international students using a shared set of questions about arrival, culture, relationships and home, with an added prompt about transportation and material conditions in the later interviews. I compared similarities and disagreements, then consulted university resources and academic literature for context.
