ENGL 1301 / Project 03 · Lubbock, Texas · 2026

Far from home.

Four interviews. One city. Different meanings of home.

I asked four international students what it feels like to settle into Lubbock. Their answers took me through familiar places—classrooms, a library, church, a workplace—and into harder questions about friendships and distance from family. This is an inquiry into how a city can become familiar before someone is ready to call it home.

FIELD NOTEThe question

Before the interviews / one question

I expected a yes or no. I heard something more complicated.

The longer I listened, the less a single definition of home seemed useful.

I began with a question: how do international students at Texas Tech adjust to Lubbock, and what makes this place feel like home? I asked four students about arrival, friendships, daily routines and belonging, then compared their answers with campus resources and published research.

Three participants are from Nigeria and one is from Ghana. They arrived by different routes, including time in Virginia, Canada and Dallas. Some were newcomers; others had years to build routines here.

These are four personal accounts, not a survey of the entire international-student community. I want to understand the differences, not flatten them into one answer.

A note from the interviewer / 01

At first I thought the question about a “second home” would get a straightforward yes or no. Instead, one student called Lubbock her third home, while another enjoyed his time in Lubbock but still did not consider it home. I realized I was asking them to fit their lives into a label that might not fit.

RESEARCH QUESTION

How do international students at Texas Tech navigate adjustment and develop a sense of belonging in Lubbock?

Four personal accounts—not a representative survey. Interviews remain the primary evidence.
01 / 05Arriving

Chapter 1 / Arriving in Lubbock

The city they imagined. The campus they met.

Participants 01 and 02 expected a nearly deserted city. Participant 03 noticed how much quieter Lubbock felt than Dallas, while Participant 04 remembered the dry, brown landscape she saw on the drive in. Their first impressions depended partly on the places they knew before Texas.

The first student noticed strangers greeting her; the second was struck by a cheerful campus. Participant 04 said TTU itself makes Lubbock feel livelier. Their answers made me separate a welcoming university from the larger city around it.

02 / 05Connecting

Chapter 2 / Finding Your People

Finding your people takes more than an invitation.

Three participants talked about the TTU library: seeing people while studying, visiting almost daily, or spending long hours working there. Participant 04 felt comfortable at her campus job, where colleagues accommodated her schedule, and also mentioned the Student Union. Their answers brought an abstract word like “belonging” down to ordinary places.

The places mattered, but so did the people in them. One participant praised supportive professors and classmates; another mentioned hackathons, church and technology workshops. The longer-term students described advisers, coworkers, church friends and the Society of Women Engineers. The kinds of support differed, even when each person felt welcomed.

Community close-up / Tree of Restoration (TOR)

What happens between Sunday and feeling known?

Participants 01 and 02 named Tree of Restoration (TOR). TOR describes teams for hospitality, worship and creative media, as well as a volunteer ride ministry. That is what the organization offers; the interviews do not establish that either student participated in those particular teams. Still, these activities show what practical welcome might look like beyond campus.

Welcome was not the whole story. Participant 02 noticed heated political conversations; Participant 03 sometimes felt outside sports discussions; and Participant 04 explained how missing shared cultural references could make friendships harder. She was describing a challenge, not saying everyone excluded her.

A familiar place offers repeated chances to meet people. But recognition does not always become a close friendship, and a supportive supervisor is not the same as family. These are interpretations of what participants reported, not scenes I personally observed.

03 / 05Movement

Chapter 3 / Life Beyond Campus

Living here depends on where you can go.

Interviews 03 and 04 both reveal a practical condition of life in Lubbock: neither participant drives—but they describe transportation differently.

Participant 03 said she has to plan outings because she cannot drive. Participant 04 also has no car, but described buses she can use for trips to campus and elsewhere. If a bus is unavailable, Uber costs money and walking may be preferable. Transportation was a constraint for one student and a mixed experience for the other.

FROM INTERVIEW 03 / ABOUT TRANSPORTATION

Participant 03 explained that without driving, even a trip to Walmart requires planning rather than simply getting in a car.

Paraphrased from Interview 03 · transportation follow-up

I had started by asking about culture and friendship. These answers reminded me that getting to an event is part of joining it. A student may know about a welcoming group but still have to consider the route, cost and time before attending.

A note from the interviewer / 02

Transportation was not where I expected this project to go. Listening to Participants 03 and 04 made me realize that asking “Where do you feel comfortable?” is different from asking “Where can you actually get to?” I didn't ask everyone the same transport follow-up, so I can't pretend their answers are directly comparable.

Texas Tech describes Citibus routes students can ride with a TTU ID and its evening on-campus Raider Ride service. Those resources matter, but published routes cannot tell me whether an individual student’s destination or schedule is covered.

I cannot tell from four interviews how common these problems are. I can say that belonging may be affected by mobility as well as social connection—and I would ask all four participants more about that if I repeated the interviews.

Four different answers to “home.”

At first, I treated the word home as though everyone would define it in the same way. The interviews showed me why that was too simple. Choose each participant to hear what belonging means in that account.

05 / 05Belonging

Chapter 5 / What I Discovered

What I discovered was not one definition of home.

Participant 01 calls Lubbock her third home after Nigeria and Virginia, where family lives. Participant 02 enjoys TTU but does not consider Lubbock his second home. He says leaving would not affect him much emotionally and describes his strong connection to family in Houston. Both described positive experiences at TTU while keeping important relationships elsewhere.

After several years, Participant 03 feels at home with friends and church but still weighs what Dallas means to her. Participant 04 makes the distinction explicit: a community can feel like home when the city itself does not. Time in a place does not automatically settle the question.

Their advice differed. One recommended events balanced with academics; another urged students to be proactive. The longer-term participants cautioned against forcing friendships or changing yourself simply to fit in. I hear a tension between reaching out and waiting for genuine connection.

Welcome, familiarity and home seem to develop at different speeds. Family distance, cultural misunderstandings and transport affect that process, even alongside positive experiences at work, school and church.

I cannot verify every interaction the students described, and four interviews cannot speak for a whole campus. Their differences leave me with a more useful question: does belonging live in a place, in people, or somewhere between them?

A note from the interviewer / 03

What stayed with me most was that the students were not simply choosing between “home” and “not home.” They were talking about different kinds of attachment—some to family, some to church, some to a workplace or study space. That feels closer to the complicated question I wanted to ask in the first place.

After the interviews / questions that remain

Three spaces.
More questions.

The interviews changed how I think about familiar places on campus. A library, a student center, and a landmark can appear welcoming in a photograph, but that doesn't tell me whether someone feels at home there. These three images help me reflect on what I learned and what the interviews could not answer.

What counts as evidence?

The accounts here come from four interviews. These campus photographs were taken in April 2022; they are archival illustrations, not observations I conducted for this project. I do not assume that a photograph can show how people feel.

01 / Everyday routines

University Library

More than one participant talked about spending time at the library. Participant 02 also described technology workshops as opportunities to meet people. The photograph shows the building, not those encounters.

What the interviews show
One location can serve different purposes: studying, following a routine, or finding a way into campus activities.
What remains unanswered
Do conversations at workshops and shared study spaces grow into friendships that last beyond the library?

02 / Familiar stops

Student Union

Participant 04 mentioned the Student Union as a place to get food. In the same conversation, she described finding support through people at her campus job. These are different kinds of connection, even when both happen at school.

What the interviews show
Being comfortable using a campus space does not necessarily mean having close relationships within it.
What remains unanswered
When does a practical stop between classes become a place where a student feels known?

03 / The places we picture

Memorial Circle

This is a recognizable campus landmark, but none of the four participants identified Memorial Circle as the place that made Lubbock feel like home. Instead, they talked about friends, church, study spaces, work and family elsewhere.

What the interviews show
The locations most visible in campus photographs may not be the ones most important to someone's sense of belonging.
What remains unanswered
Which everyday places would students choose to photograph if they were telling their own stories of home?

A limit of this inquiry

A place is visible.
Belonging isn't.

These pictures locate the stories, but the interviews give them meaning. Four conversations also leave important perspectives out. I would want to hear from students with different lengths of time in Lubbock, backgrounds and living arrangements before drawing broader conclusions.

For now, the question I am left with is not simply where international students spend time, but which places let them move from being present to feeling connected.

Research method / what I can and cannot conclude

How I made this project.

I interviewed four international students using a shared set of questions about arrival, culture, relationships and home, with an added prompt about transportation and material conditions in the later interviews. I compared similarities and disagreements, then consulted university resources and academic literature for context.

01

Firsthand evidence

Four student interviews, about 24 minutes combined. Three students are originally from Nigeria and one from Ghana. Some are relatively new to TTU; others have spent several semesters building networks. Names are not displayed.

PRIMARY
02

Multimodal choices

Four original audio files let visitors hear participants directly; expandable transcripts provide a readable alternative; credited campus images locate the project; interactive buttons compare four participants’ perspectives. The further questions prepares a future campus visit; it is not a record of completed observation.

MEDIA
03

Limitations

Three participants are from Nigeria and one from Ghana. The sample is small. Interviews 03–04 were edited from automatic captions and are not verbatim. Archival photographs are illustrations, not firsthand observations; audio-publication permission was confirmed by the interviewer; permission to use TOR's official photographs for this project has also been confirmed. Original observation notes have not been claimed where none were recorded.

SCOPE

FAR FROM HOME.

International student experiences at Texas Tech University. An independent student project for ENGL 1301.

About this project

Primary evidence: four interviews with TTU international students, approximately 24 minutes combined. This inquiry explores individual experiences without making claims about all international students.
